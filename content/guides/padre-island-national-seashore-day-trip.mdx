Drive south on Park Road 22, past the last condos on the Island, and the houses run out. Soon it's dunes, grasslands, and a long, empty line of Gulf beach. That's Padre Island National Seashore. It's a national park, and it's right at the edge of Corpus Christi. The park protects about 66 miles of undeveloped beach, and you can get to the most popular part of it in a regular car, with a cooler, a baby, and too many sand toys.

This guide covers how to plan an easy family day there, plus what you need to know if you want to go farther down the beach.

## First, which Padre Island?

Visitors mix this up all the time, so it's worth saying. The national seashore sits on the northern part of Padre Island, just south of Corpus Christi. The resort town of South Padre Island is a different place, about 100 miles south near Brownsville. A man-made channel cuts the island in two, so you can't drive from the seashore down to South Padre on the sand. That trip means heading back inland, a drive the park puts at three to four hours.

## Getting there from Corpus Christi

Take South Padre Island Drive (TX-358) east over the JFK Causeway onto the Island. The road becomes Park Road 22. Keep going south through the residential and resort part of the Island until you reach the park entrance station.

The Malaquite Visitor Center is the hub of a family day, about 15 minutes past the entrance station. Its address is 20420 Park Road 22. The park warns that GPS and map apps sometimes drop that address several miles off, so search for "Malaquite Visitor Center" by name instead. For a sense of distance, the park lists the visitor center as about 38 miles and 45 minutes from Corpus Christi International Airport.

There's no public transportation to the park, so you'll need to drive.

## Entrance passes

Every visitor needs an entrance pass, all year, for every part of the park. A vehicle pass covers everyone riding in your car.

- **One-day vehicle pass:** $10. Good until midnight on the day you buy it.
- **Seven-day vehicle pass:** $25. You need this one, or an annual pass, if you're staying overnight.
- **Motorcycle:** $5 for one day, $20 for seven days.
- **Walking or biking in:** $5 per person for one day, $15 for seven days. Kids 15 and under are free.
- **Padre Island annual pass:** $45. It covers you and your passengers for a year from the month you buy it.
- **America the Beautiful passes**, including the senior, military, access, and fourth-grade versions, are accepted here too.

You can buy a pass at the entrance station or the visitor center, with cash or card, or ahead of time online through the federal recreation site. Your city of Corpus Christi beach parking permit doesn't work in the national seashore, and you don't need one there.

Camping and the boat ramp cost extra. Entrance passes don't cover them.

## Hours

The park itself is open 24 hours a day, every day of the year. The entrance station's hours vary from day to day. If you arrive when nobody is in the booth, the park says to drive in and pay as you leave if it's open then.

The **Malaquite Visitor Center** is open 9 a.m. to 5 p.m. every day except Thanksgiving and Christmas.

## The easy family day: Malaquite Beach

If you have little kids, this is the part of the park to know. **Malaquite Beach** is a 4.5-mile stretch in front of the visitor center, and it's closed to beach driving. You don't have trucks rolling past your blanket, which is a big deal when you've got a toddler who runs for the water. The park calls it ideal for families looking for a car-free beach.

The visitor center complex makes a day here easy:

- A big paved parking lot, which the park says holds more than 800 cars
- Restrooms across the deck from the visitor center, open around the clock, plus more restrooms at the north end of the parking lot
- Cold-water showers indoors, and outdoor rinse-off showers along the boardwalk and at the north end of the lot
- Shaded picnic tables on the deck, and a covered picnic area with a charcoal grill at the north end of the lot
- An exhibit area about the island's history, plants, and animals, plus a park film with captions
- A park store with snacks, ice, sunscreen, bug spray, and books
- Junior Ranger booklets for older kids
- A wooden boardwalk, and an accessible ramp from the deck down to the sand

The visitor center lends beach wheelchairs and a beach walker for free, first come, first served, daily from 9 a.m. to 4:45 p.m. The wheelchairs aren't self-propelled, so someone has to push. Wheelchairs on sand are a big help for a grandparent who wants to get down to the water with the grandkids.

## Swimming and safety

There are **no lifeguards anywhere in the national seashore.** You swim at your own risk. Swimming and wading are allowed along the Gulf at North Beach, Malaquite Beach, and South Beach, and in the calm, shallow Laguna Madre at Bird Island Basin.

The park warns about:

- **Rip currents**, plus currents that run along the beach and sudden drop-offs. The National Weather Service posts a daily rip current risk in its surf zone forecast, so check it the morning you go. If you get caught in a rip, don't fight it. Swim parallel to shore until you're out of it, then swim in. If you can't, float or tread water and wave for help.
- **Jellyfish and Portuguese man-of-war.** The park's first-aid advice for a jellyfish sting is to rinse with seawater, test vinegar on a small spot, and soak in vinegar if it helps or switch to hot water if it makes things worse. Then remove any tentacles you can see.
- **Stingrays.** A stingray wound gets hot water, as hot as the person can stand, for 30 to 90 minutes.
- **Rattlesnakes** in the dunes and grasslands. Stay on trails and boardwalks, and stay off the dunes.
- **Mosquitoes and ticks.** Bring repellent and check the kids, and yourself, afterward.

Cell service in the park is unreliable. Tell someone your plan, and know that in an emergency you call 911 or flag down a ranger.

With a baby along, two more tips that aren't park rules, just South Texas common sense: There's very little natural shade on a Gulf beach, so bring your own canopy or umbrella. And the wind out here is no joke, so stake it down.

## Beach driving and mile markers

Beach driving is one of the park's biggest draws. Texas beaches count as public highways, so traffic laws apply, seat belts included.

- **North Beach** is a 1.5-mile stretch north of Malaquite Beach, reached from a turnoff about a mile past the park entrance sign. Driving is allowed when conditions permit, and four-wheel drive is always recommended.
- **South Beach** starts at the end of the paved road, about half a mile south of the visitor center. It runs roughly 60 miles to the Mansfield Channel. Mile marker signs stand every five miles.
- **Two-wheel drive** vehicles are allowed only on North Beach and on the first five miles of South Beach (mile 0 to 5). Even there, the park says four-wheel drive may be required depending on conditions.
- **Past mile 5, four-wheel drive is required.** That's the stretch that leads to Little Shell and Big Shell beaches, the Yarborough Pass camping area (about 15 miles down, then a 1.6-mile high-clearance road), and the Mansfield Channel.
- **Speed limits:** 15 mph year-round from mile 0 to 2.5. From mile 2.5 to 60, it's 15 mph from March 1 through Labor Day and 25 mph from the day after Labor Day through February.
- **Rules of the sand:** northbound traffic has the right of way. Keep the main driving lane clear when you park, and never drive on the dunes, on vegetation, or on the tidal flats.
- **Vehicles must be street legal.** Unregistered ATVs, UTVs, golf carts, and dune buggies are prohibited. Big two-wheel-drive RVs are a bad idea on the sand.

Two warnings from the park are worth repeating. First, the park doesn't tow private vehicles, and a private tow from down-island can cost hundreds to thousands of dollars. Second, a predicted high tide of 1.5 feet or more can push water right up to the dunes in places. Check the tide predictions and the forecast before you go. Bring a shovel, traction boards, a spare, a jump kit, extra water and food, and a full tank of gas.

For a family day with a little one, the easiest plan is to park at the visitor center and stay on Malaquite. Save the down-island adventure for when the kids are older.

## Sea turtle hatchling releases (summer)

The park's sea turtle program is famous. Kemp's ridley sea turtles nest at Padre Island National Seashore more than anywhere else in the United States. In summer, the park invites the public to watch hatchlings crawl to the Gulf.

How it works:

- **Season:** typically mid-June through August. The 2026 season is over, and the park says to check back in June 2027.
- **When and where:** most releases happen around sunrise, about 6:45 a.m., on Malaquite Beach in front of the visitor center.
- **Short notice:** hatching is hard to predict, so releases are usually announced only the day before. They show up on the park's hatchling release webpage, its social media, and a recorded **Hatchling Hotline at (361) 949-7163**, which you can call any time.
- **Cancellations:** releases get canceled for weather, high tides, flooded roads, or turtles that aren't ready. Call the hotline before you leave home.
- **Buy your pass that morning, not the night before.** A one-day pass bought the evening before expires at midnight.

When you're there, skip white clothing, since it can confuse the hatchlings. Turn off every flash and light, leave food in the car (it draws gulls), stay behind the barriers, and stay out of the water near the release. If a wave washes in, plant your feet and stand still. Pets have to stay home for releases. Crowds average around 1,300 people, more on weekends, so get there early.

Spotted a nesting turtle or hatchlings on your own? Don't touch them. Report them right away to a ranger or the Texas sea turtle hotline at 1-866-TURTLE-5 (1-866-887-8535). Nesting season runs roughly April through August, so drivers should keep an eye out on the sand.

## Fishing from the beach

Surf fishing here is a classic, and the Laguna side at Bird Island Basin is popular too. Anyone 17 or older needs a valid Texas fishing license with the saltwater endorsement, and **you can't buy one inside the park**, so get it before you head down. Kids under 17 don't need a license. State fishing rules apply, along with a few federal ones. Digging for bait is prohibited, and so is fishing in designated swimming areas. With kids in the water, cast well away from the swimmers in front of the visitor center.

## Pets

The seashore is dog-friendly. Leashed pets (6-foot leash maximum) are allowed on the beaches, campgrounds, trails, and boardwalks. They aren't allowed in park buildings, and aggressive or disruptive pets should stay off the visitor center deck, the boardwalk, and the beach right in front of the visitor center. Clean up after your dog, and never leave a pet in the car.

The park has a standing alert that canine distemper is believed to be present among its wild animals. Make sure your dog is vaccinated, or leave it home. Coyotes live on the island too.

## Food, gas, and supplies

Plan to be self-sufficient. **There's no gas, EV charging, restaurant, grocery store, or medical facility in the park.** The park store sells snacks, ice, sunscreen, and bug spray, and that's about it for food. The park says the nearest services are about 13 miles north, back on the Island.

Fill up before you drive in, and pack a real cooler with lunch, snacks, and more water than you think you need. Baby gear goes in too: wipes, a change of clothes, and something for shade.

For dinner on the way home, **Doc's Seafood & Steaks** is a waterfront seafood-and-steak restaurant under the JFK Causeway bridge on the Island side. Doc's is our family's go-to, and it's an easy stop on the drive back into town. Peak season runs Memorial Day through Labor Day, and Doc's says it can't take reservations then, so plan for a wait on summer weekends.

## Camping (briefly)

To stretch the day into a weekend, the park has two first-come, first-served campgrounds with no reservations and no hookups. **Malaquite Campground** sits near the visitor center on the Gulf side, at $14 a night. **Bird Island Basin**, on the Laguna Madre, is $8 a night. Primitive camping is allowed on North Beach, South Beach, and at Yarborough Pass, and it's included with a seven-day or annual pass. Camping isn't allowed on Malaquite Beach. Overnight stays need a seven-day or annual pass, not the one-day pass.

## A few more things to do

- **Grasslands Nature Trail:** a paved, wheelchair-accessible 0.75-mile loop just south of the entrance station, through dunes and coastal prairie. It's stroller-friendly, with a few benches.
- **Bird Island Basin:** the calm, shallow Laguna Madre side, popular with birders, kayakers, and windsurfers.
- **Beachcombing:** shells and sea beans wash up all along the beach.
- **Bob Hall Pier, on the way down:** this Nueces County pier sits in Padre Balli Park, outside the national seashore, and reopened in 2026 after its rebuild. It's an easy stop to break up the drive and stretch your legs. The county charges its own fees, separate from your park pass.
- **Ranger programs:** talks on the visitor center deck at different times of year, with more ranger-led programs in summer.

## Local tips

- **Check conditions the morning you go.** Look at the park's alerts, the tide predictions, and the rip current forecast.
- **Arrive early in summer.** The visitor center lot is big, but mornings are cooler and calmer with kids.
- **Bring cash or a card for the entrance**, or buy the pass online first to skip the line.
- **Download anything you need before you go.** Don't count on your phone working once you're past the entrance.
